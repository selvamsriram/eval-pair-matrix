## What changed and why

The original audit compared each judge's diagonal cell against that judge's scores on **other generators' answers** — different answers with different difficulty, length and refusal behaviour. This version makes the comparison **within the same answer**: every candidate answer was judged by all three judges, so for each answer we compare the matching judge against the mean of the two non-matching judges. Deltas are in percentage points; positive means the matching (same-deployment) judge flags *more* than the cross judges.

**Decision rule outcome (per the review's Step 4 rule):**

- **Recall: null.** Holding the answer fixed, the matching judge is *not* worse at catching genuine induced errors (Δ = -0.5 pp, 95% CI [-2.7, +1.7] — includes 0). The headline “self-leniency lets a judge miss its own model's errors” claim is **not** supported once generator difficulty is controlled.
- **False-positive rate: robust effect.** On gold-negative answers the matching judge has a lower FPR than the cross judges (Δ = -4.3 pp, CI [-6.6, -2.0] — excludes 0). Same-deployment leniency surfaces as **reduced over-flagging of its own model's correct answers**, not as missed true errors.
- **Overall flag rate: robust effect** (Δ = -2.2 pp, CI [-3.8, -0.6] — excludes 0), driven by the FPR gap.

**Suggested headline sentence for the abstract:**

> Across a fully crossed 3×3 matrix, raw diagonal differences are small (mean diagonal F1 84.378 vs off-diagonal 83.431). After a paired, answer-level analysis on 275 validated records, we find **no robust same-deployment effect on recall** (Δ = -0.5 pp, 95% CI [-2.7, +1.7]), but a **robust same-deployment leniency on gold-negative answers** (false-positive-rate Δ = -4.3 pp, CI [-6.6, -2.0]); generator behaviour and answerability explain substantial variation in judge performance.
